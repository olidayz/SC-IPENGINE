# 4B-CHARACTERS

Stage 2a of visual development. Apply the VLD to people — what they look like, what they wear, what they carry.

Runs in parallel with 4c-locations.md. Both constrained by the approved VLD from 4a.

---

## Anti-Patterns (Read First)

Kill any character visual package that matches these before presenting:

| Anti-Pattern | Signal | Why It Dies |
|-------------|--------|------------|
| **The Stock Photo** | Character looks like a generic version of their archetype. The Tough Cop. The Corporate Villain. The Outsider Protagonist. | Find the specific visual detail that makes them THIS character in THIS world. Archetype is a starting point — specificity is the destination. |
| **The Costume Change** | Character's visual design doesn't connect to their psychology. Clothes are cool but meaningless. | Clothing is character. Every garment choice should tell you something about their wound, their aspiration, or their performance. If you can't explain WHY they wear it, it's decoration. |
| **The Uniform** | All characters from the same faction look identical. No individual expression. | Faction provides the base. Individual expression provides the variation. The GRADIENT between group identity and personal identity IS the visual interest. |
| **The Mannequin** | Character is described as a static image — no sense of how they move, occupy space, or relate to environments. | Characters are bodies in motion. A visual package that only describes what someone looks like standing still misses half the design. |
| **The Accessory Pile** | Character is over-designed — too many signature details, props, and visual gimmicks competing for attention. | One signature detail, clearly hierarchized. Everything else supports. If everything is special, nothing is. |

### Process Anti-Pattern

| Anti-Pattern | Signal | Why It Dies |
|-------------|--------|------------|
| **The Open Kitchen** | Presenting principle analysis, generation prompts, evaluation tables to the user | The user sees character visual packages, not the generation process. The output is the design, not the methodology. |

## The 6 Principles of Character Visual Design

### Principle 1: SILHOUETTE FIRST

*Recognition at distance. The shape IS the character.*

If you can't identify this character as a 50px thumbnail, the design hasn't done its job. Silhouette is the primary visual identifier — it survives distance, darkness, and motion blur.

| Prompt | Ask the Character |
|--------|------------------|
| **Shape Language** | Is this character angular or curved? Compact or expansive? Vertical or horizontal? The geometric DNA of their silhouette. |
| **Distinction** | Could you tell this character apart from every other character in the story by silhouette alone? If two characters share a silhouette, one needs redesigning. |
| **Thumbnail Test** | Shrink the character to the size of a phone notification icon. Can you still tell who it is? What survives at minimum resolution? |

**Benchmark:** *Spider-Verse* — every variant Spider-Person has a completely distinct silhouette. Miles, Gwen, Noir, Peni, Ham — recognizable at any size. The silhouette IS the character promise.

### Principle 2: CLOTHING AS WOUND

*Costume is psychology externalized. What someone wears reveals what they're hiding, performing, or protecting.*

Clothing choices are character choices. Every garment is a decision the character made (or had made for them), and that decision reveals something about who they are.

| Prompt | Ask the Character |
|--------|------------------|
| **Performance** | What is this character PERFORMING with their clothing? Authority? Normalcy? Wealth? Indifference? What audience are they dressing for? |
| **Protection** | What is this character HIDING or SHIELDING with their clothing? The wound they don't want seen? The identity they're suppressing? |
| **States** | Does this character have multiple costume states? On-duty vs. off-duty? Public vs. private? Formal vs. crisis? How do the states differ, and what does the difference reveal? |

**Benchmark:** *Succession* — every Roy's wardrobe is a wound made visible. Kendall's try-hard luxury (performance of competence). Shiv's power blazers (performance of authority she doesn't have). Roman's casual sloppiness (performance of not caring). Connor's outdated formality (performance of relevance). The clothes ARE the character study.

### Principle 3: FACTION GRADIENT

*Characters exist on a spectrum between their group's visual identity and their individual expression.*

The gradient between faction conformity and personal deviation tells you everything about a character's relationship to power, belonging, and rebellion.

| Prompt | Ask the Character |
|--------|------------------|
| **Compliance** | How closely does this character match their faction's visual standards? Full compliance (true believer), slight deviation (comfortable insider), visible divergence (restless member), rejection (defector)? |
| **Direction** | If they deviate, WHICH direction? Toward another faction? Toward individuality? Toward a past identity? The direction of deviation reveals aspiration or nostalgia. |
| **Cost** | What does deviation cost in this world? In some worlds, a wrong color gets you killed. In others, it gets you a sideways look. The stakes of visual nonconformity. |

**Benchmark:** *The Handmaid's Tale* — the faction gradient is literally color-coded (red, blue, green, grey, black). But the compelling characters are the ones who introduce micro-deviations: Offred's hidden contraband, Serena's jewelry as residual vanity. The deviations are the character.

### Principle 4: VISUAL ARC

*If the character transforms, the appearance must transform. Act 3 should be inevitable in retrospect from Act 1.*

Character arcs are visible. The visual arc tracks internal change through external appearance — not just a "makeover moment" but a gradual, motivated shift.

| Prompt | Ask the Character |
|--------|------------------|
| **Start State** | What does this character look like at the story's beginning? What does their appearance communicate about who they think they are? |
| **End State** | What do they look like at the story's end? What changed, and what was always there underneath? |
| **Continuity** | What visual element DOESN'T change across the arc? The thread of continuity — the thing that was always true about them, even when everything else shifted. |

**Benchmark:** *Breaking Bad* — Walter White's visual arc is one of the great character designs in television. Beige khakis and ill-fitting shirts (invisible man) → black hat and sunglasses (Heisenberg performance) → the goatee stays throughout (the danger was always there). The visual continuity IS the thesis: he didn't become Heisenberg, he always was.

### Principle 5: DETAIL HIERARCHY

*The one thing you notice first, second, third. Every character has a signature detail.*

Visual attention is hierarchical. Good character design controls what the eye sees in order — the macro shape (silhouette), the color impression, then the ONE detail that defines this person.

| Prompt | Ask the Character |
|--------|------------------|
| **Signature** | What's the one detail that IS this character? Not the most visually loud — the most meaningful. The thing that, once you notice it, changes how you read everything else. |
| **Supporting** | What details support the signature without competing? The secondary visual elements that reinforce the character's world position. |
| **Absence** | What's conspicuously MISSING? What would a person in this role normally have that this character doesn't? Absence is a detail too. |

**Benchmark:** *No Country for Old Men* — Chigurh's detail hierarchy: first the silhouette (tall, heavy, wrong), then the haircut (alien, inhuman, time-displaced), then the captive bolt pistol (the specific wrongness of the weapon). Each detail compounds. 

### Principle 6: BODY IN SPACE

*How they occupy rooms. Who shrinks, who expands, who leans. Physicality as character.*

Character design isn't just what someone looks like standing still — it's how they move through, occupy, and relate to the environments they're in. A character who looks wrong in their space is telling you something.

| Prompt | Ask the Character |
|--------|------------------|
| **Posture** | How does this character carry themselves? Upright or hunched? Open or closed? Does their posture change depending on who's watching? |
| **Scale Relationship** | How does this character relate to the spaces they inhabit? Do they fill rooms or get swallowed by them? Are they comfortable or displaced? |
| **Environmental Fit** | Does this character BELONG in their primary environment? A character who looks "right" in their space is settled. A character who looks "wrong" is in tension with their world. The mismatch IS the story. |

**Benchmark:** *Lost in Translation* — Bob Harris is too big for every space in Tokyo. Chairs, doorways, bath tubs, karaoke booths — his body doesn't fit. Charlotte is physically small but visually present — she fills space with attention rather than mass. Their physical relationship to the city IS the film's emotional thesis: displacement, scale, not-belonging.

## Generation Process

### Step 1: Identify and NAME Key Characters

From the story window: protagonist, opponent, 1-4 ensemble. 3-6 total. Only characters with narrative weight get full visual packages.

**Every character gets a proper name.** Not "the cowboy," not "the protagonist," not "the leader." A name. First name minimum. Full name if the world supports it. The name should feel native to the world — era, culture, faction, class all inform naming.

| Naming Rule | Why |
|-------------|-----|
| **Name, not role** | "Mara Voss" carries into every prompt downstream. "The rebel leader" doesn't. |
| **World-native** | Names should sound like they belong in THIS world. Faction, class, region inform naming conventions. |
| **Sayable** | The Say Test from Stage 1 applies here. If you can't say it in conversation, rename. |
| **Distinct** | No two characters should share starting sounds or syllable count. Mara and Marcus compete. Mara and Jude don't. |
| **Carry forward** | Once named, the name is used in ALL downstream prompts, scene descriptions, and shot references. Never revert to role labels. |

### Step 1.5: Character Read (Before Visual Design)

For each character, extract or infer psychological material from world build + story window. Don't design from faction membership alone — design from inner life.

| Question | Source | If Missing |
|----------|--------|------------|
| **What do they WANT?** | Story window (protagonist/opponent explicit) | Infer from faction goals + story role |
| **What do they NEED?** | Story window wound (protagonist explicit) | Infer from faction position — what does this role deny them? |
| **What are they PERFORMING?** | Faction role + world position | Ask: what does this character want others to see? |
| **What are they HIDING?** | The gap between want and need | Ask: what truth about themselves would damage them if visible? |
| **What's their RELATIONSHIP to the symbol?** | World build — how the world's central tension lives in this person | Ask: are they a believer, a skeptic, a user, or a casualty? |

For ensemble characters with thin story window entries, infer from their faction position + their role in the story's pressure structure. **Flag the inference** — it's a design hypothesis, not canon. The user can correct before proceeding.

**The read is internal.** The user sees the resulting visual design, not the psych extraction. But the extraction must happen BEFORE any visual decisions — otherwise you're decorating, not designing.

### Step 2: For Each Character — Run the Principles

Work through the 6 principles in order. The VLD constrains every decision — palette must come from the VLD's color system, materials from the VLD's texture rules, lighting from the VLD's philosophy. The Character Read constrains every decision — costume must come from psychology, not aesthetics alone.

**Generation order per character:**

1. **Silhouette** — shape language, distinction from other characters
2. **Faction Gradient** — where they sit on the compliance spectrum
3. **Clothing as Wound** — what they wear and why, multiple states if relevant
4. **Detail Hierarchy** — the signature detail, supporting details, conspicuous absences
5. **Visual Arc** — start state, end state, continuity thread
6. **Body in Space** — how they relate to their primary environments

### Step 3: Ensemble Coherence Check (Internal)

Do the characters work as a visual system? Test:
- Can you distinguish all characters by silhouette alone?
- Do faction groupings read visually?
- Is there enough contrast between characters in the same scene?
- Does the protagonist's visual identity carry the most weight?

### Step 4: Gate Check (Internal — Do Not Present)

Run each character against quality gates. Fix failures before presenting.

## Output Structure

Stage 2 character output uses the two-layer structure defined in SKILL.md:

### Layer 1: Character Overview Table (present FIRST)

Scannable checkpoint + visual sampling. One row per character. User reviews this before proceeding. The Image Prompt column contains a ready-to-use generation prompt.

```
| Character | Role | Silhouette | Palette | Signature Detail | Visual Arc | Image Prompt |
|-----------|------|-----------|---------|-----------------|------------|-------------|
| **[Name]** | [position in world + story] | [shape language, thumbnail] | [color range + arc] | [the ONE thing] | [start → end, continuity thread] | [Two-panel split frame prompt. LEFT close-up: appearance, costume, signature detail, lighting, VLD style anchor. RIGHT wide: body in primary environment, posture, scale, VLD composition and framing. Self-contained, 120-150 words, includes anti-prompt.] |
```

### Layer 2: Full Character Visual Packages

One per character. Complete depth — every principle covered.

```
## [CHARACTER NAME]

**Role:** [one-line: position in world + position in story]
**Faction:** [faction allegiance + gradient position]

**Silhouette:** [shape language, geometric DNA, thumbnail description]

**Palette:** [personal color range within VLD system — how they fit or contrast]

**Costume Logic:**
- **Primary state:** [what they wear most, and WHY]
- **Secondary state:** [if applicable — off-duty, formal, crisis]
- **What the clothing says:** [the wound or performance the costume reveals]

**Signature Detail:** [the ONE thing — prop, scar, accessory, habit, absence]

**Supporting Details:** [2-3 secondary visual elements]

**Body in Space:** [posture, scale relationship, environmental fit/misfit]

**Visual Arc:** [start → end, with continuity thread]
```

## Quality Gates (Run Silently)

| Gate | Test | Kill If |
|------|------|---------|
| **Taste Filter** | Does this character pass Daniel's aesthetic PASS/FAIL? | Generic, bland, seen-before, costume-drama energy |
| **VLD Compliance** | Does the character's visual design follow the VLD's rules? | Colors, materials, or lighting that violate the established visual language |
| **Specificity** | Is this character visually specific to THIS world? | Could exist in any noir / any sci-fi / any corporate drama |
| **Silhouette Distinction** | Can you distinguish this character from all others by shape alone? | Two characters with interchangeable silhouettes |
| **Wound Legibility** | Does the visual design reveal something about the character's psychology? | Costume is cool but psychologically empty |
| **Principle Coverage** | Are all 6 principles addressed? | Any principle completely skipped = incomplete design |

**If all pass:** Present character visual packages.

**If any fail:** Fix silently, then present.

# Vis-Dev References

Visual benchmarks organized by principle. Use during generation to calibrate quality. Each reference specifies what to TAKE and what to LEAVE.

---

## Art Director Principles

### Contrast System

| Reference | Take | Leave |
|-----------|------|-------|
| *Parasite* (Bong Joon-ho) | Spatial contrast as class system — above/below, sun/rain, clean/stained. The contrast IS the thesis. | The thriller mechanics. We want the visual system, not the genre. |
| *Succession* (Mark Mylod, Andrij Parekh) | Wealth vs. decay — pristine surfaces hiding dysfunction. The desaturation itself is a contrast against how rich the world actually is. | The handheld wouldn't work for all worlds. Take the palette logic, not the camera behavior. |
| *Spider-Verse* (Sony Animation) | Visual contrast between variants — same concept, radically different execution. Dimension-hopping as palette-shifting. | The comic-book aesthetic is specific to that IP. Take the SYSTEM of contrast, not the style. |

**Negative Benchmark:** *Tron: Legacy* — one contrast (light/dark, blue/orange) applied uniformly. No variation, no gradient, no violation. Beautiful but monotonous. The contrast system has no GRAMMAR — it's a single word.

### Constraint as Identity

| Reference | Take | Leave |
|-----------|------|-------|
| *Wes Anderson* (entire filmography) | Symmetry as absolute constraint. The constraint IS the authorship. Every deviation from symmetry carries narrative weight. | The whimsy. Anderson's constraint works because of his tone — don't transplant tone, transplant the PRINCIPLE of rigorous visual rules. |
| *Kubrick* (entire filmography) | One-point perspective as constraint. Corridors, hallways, tables — everything converges. The constraint creates dread through geometry. | The pace. Kubrick's visual constraints serve slow-burn storytelling. The constraint principle is portable; the tempo isn't. |
| *The Handmaid's Tale* (Colin Watkinson) | Color-coding as absolute constraint. Red, blue, green, grey — no exceptions. The constraint IS the world's totalitarianism. | The didacticism. The color system works because it's embedded, not explained. |

**Negative Benchmark:** *Generic cyberpunk* — rain + neon + Asian signage. No actual constraints — everything is permitted as long as it "looks cyberpunk." A mood, not a system. Swap any element and nothing breaks because nothing was load-bearing.

### Emotional Cartography

| Reference | Take | Leave |
|-----------|------|-------|
| *Succession* (Mark Mylod) | Distinct visual modes for distinct emotional registers. Corporate scenes: architectural, cold, steady. Emotional breakdowns: warmer, messy, handheld. Location shifts (New York → Tuscany → Norway) recalibrate the entire palette. | Don't assume all worlds need the "prestige TV" register set. Map the registers to THIS world's emotional range. |
| *Moonlight* (James Laxton) | Three-act color shift as emotional evolution. Blue moonlight (childhood), warm gold (adolescence), neon/fluorescent (adulthood). The palette ages with the character. | The naturalism. Moonlight's registers are subtle. Some worlds need harder visual shifts between modes. |
| *Everything Everywhere All at Once* (Larkin Seiple) | Radical register shifts — laundromat realism, martial arts spectacle, surreal abstraction, quiet devastation. All in the same film, all coherent. | The chaos. EEAAO makes register shifts feel earned through emotional logic. Without that, rapid shifts feel random. |

**Negative Benchmark:** *Zack Snyder's desaturation* — one visual mode (desaturated, high contrast, slow motion) applied to every emotional register. Joy, grief, rage, wonder all look the same. The emotional cartography is a single coordinate.

### Material Truthfulness

| Reference | Take | Leave |
|-----------|------|-------|
| *Blade Runner 2049* (Dennis Gassner, Roger Deakins) | Material specificity per faction. Wallace Corp: water, wood, zero dust. Orphanage: rust, ash, corroded metal. K's apartment: worn synthetic, clean but exhausted. Every material tells the power structure. | The scale. Not every world needs architectural megastructure. Take the PRINCIPLE of faction-specific materials, not the size. |
| *Chernobyl* (Luke Hull) | Material frozen in time. Soviet concrete, 1960s instrumentation, then radiation. The mundanity of the materials IS the horror — this was built to be permanent. | The period specificity. Take the principle that materials carry temporal information, not the 1980s USSR aesthetic. |
| *Mad Max: Fury Road* (Colin Gibson) | Improvised materials — everything repurposed, nothing new. The material scarcity IS the world's economy. You can read the resource crisis in every surface. | The post-apocalyptic genre assumptions. Not all material scarcity looks like a junkyard. Corporate austerity (Succession's empty rooms) is also material truthfulness. |

**Negative Benchmark:** *Generic "grunge" textures* — rust and dirt applied uniformly with no logic. No wear PATTERN — just wear. No material STORY — just distress. The visual equivalent of putting a filter on a photo and calling it art direction.

### Legible Distance

| Reference | Take | Leave |
|-----------|------|-------|
| *Mad Max: Fury Road* (John Seale) | Works at every distance. Wide: dust storms, convoy silhouettes. Medium: War Rig, pole cats. Close: muzzle, prosthetic, steering wheel brand. Every scale tells the story. | The action pacing. The legible distance principle is about design completeness, not speed. |
| *Wes Anderson* (Robert Yeoman) | Obsessive attention at every scale. Wide: dollhouse architecture. Medium: character blocking. Close: handwritten notes, miniature details. Nothing is left to chance at any distance. | The stylization level. Most worlds need less visual control than Anderson exerts. Take the discipline, not the degree. |

**Negative Benchmark:** *Many prestige TV dramas* — beautiful establishing shots (the skyline, the house, the office) but interchangeable close-ups. The texture that makes a world specific often exists at the CLOSE distance — the labels, the wear, the specific objects on a desk.

### Light as Narrator

| Reference | Take | Leave |
|-----------|------|-------|
| *Zodiac* (Harris Savides) | Light as institutional control. Newsroom: flat fluorescent, everything exposed. Basements: single-source, deep shadow. The institution shows you what it wants you to see. | The period specificity. Take the principle that light sources reveal power structures, not the 1970s palette. |
| *Sicario* (Roger Deakins) | Light as environmental threat. The tunnel sequence: total darkness to green night-vision. The border crossing: magic hour to sodium streetlight. Light marks the boundary between safety and danger. | The military context. The principle of light-as-boundary applies to corporate, domestic, and fantasy worlds too. |
| *Barry Lyndon* (John Alcott, Kubrick) | Natural/candlelight as temporal anchor. The light sources ARE the period. No artificial illumination in the 18th century sequences — the constraint created the beauty. | The historical literalism. The principle: the light source should be motivated by the world's actual infrastructure, not by what "looks good." |

**Negative Benchmark:** *Standard three-point lighting* — key, fill, back. Looks "professional" but tells you nothing about the world. Light that exists only to illuminate is a missed narrative opportunity.

### Time Signature

| Reference | Take | Leave |
|-----------|------|-------|
| *Blade Runner* (Lawrence G. Paull) | Radical era-mixing. Mayan pyramid architecture with neon advertisements. 1940s noir detective in a 2019 megacity. The temporal collision IS the aesthetic. | The specific combination. Every world has its own temporal layers — don't default to "retro-future." |
| *The Grand Budapest Hotel* (Adam Stockhausen) | Three distinct eras, each with its own complete visual system. 1930s: pastel opulence. 1960s: muted institutional. 1980s: decayed Soviet. The same building, three different films. | Anderson's specific palette choices. Take the STRUCTURE: same physical space, multiple temporal visual systems coexisting. |
| *Dark* (Netflix) | Three timelines (1953, 1986, 2019) in the same locations. The visual language shifts through material age, lighting technology, and color temperature. You know WHEN you are from the surfaces. | The time-travel mechanics. Not every world has literal time layers. But every world has eras coexisting in its built environment. |

**Negative Benchmark:** *"Futuristic" as aesthetic* — everything new, everything clean, everything glowing. No temporal depth. A world with no visible past has no gravity. The Time Signature grounds a world in consequence.

## Character Principles

### Benchmark Quick-Reference

| Principle | Positive Benchmark | What to Take |
|-----------|--------------------|--------------|
| Silhouette First | *Spider-Verse* variants | Every character distinct at any resolution |
| Clothing as Wound | *Succession* wardrobes | Every garment is a psychological tell |
| Faction Gradient | *The Handmaid's Tale* color system | Micro-deviations from faction uniform = character |
| Visual Arc | *Breaking Bad* Walter White | Appearance tracks transformation; continuity thread persists |
| Detail Hierarchy | *No Country for Old Men* Chigurh | One detail at a time, each compounding |
| Body in Space | *Lost in Translation* Bob/Charlotte | Physical relationship to environment IS emotional thesis |

## Location Principles

### Benchmark Quick-Reference

| Principle | Positive Benchmark | What to Take |

| Lived-In Test | *The Wire* Major Crimes detail | Designed from inside out, not outside in |

| Threshold Moment | *Parasite* staircase descent | Transition between spaces as cinematic event |
| Sensory Depth | *The Bear* kitchen | Sound, smell, heat, texture — a sensorium |

## Cross-Principle Patterns

Some visual problems are best solved by combining multiple principles:

| Problem | Principle Combination | How They Interact |
|---------|----------------------|-------------------|
| "This world feels generic" | Constraint as Identity + Material Truthfulness | Define what's FORBIDDEN (constraint) and make every surface SPECIFIC (material). Generic worlds lack both rules and texture. |
| "All locations look the same" | Faction Signature + History Layer + Scale as Emotion | Different factions, different pasts, different power ratios. Three axes of variation. |
| "Characters are interchangeable" | Silhouette First + Faction Gradient + Detail Hierarchy | Shape, group allegiance, and one telling detail. Three levels of distinction. |
| "The visual language doesn't support the story's range" | Emotional Cartography + Light as Narrator | Map the story's registers to visual treatments, and use light shifts to mark transitions. |
| "It looks right but feels flat" | Sensory Depth + Lived-In Test + Time Signature | Add the other senses, add evidence of life, add temporal depth. Flatness = missing dimensions. |

# 5e — COUNTER-NARRATIVE (Invocation 5)

Every world has people who push back. This invocation generates scenes from the friction — the people who hate the premise, mourn what was lost, see through the system, refuse the mythology, or profit from it with full awareness of the cost. Counter-narrative scenes are the test of the world's integrity. A world that can absorb its own criticism is a world that's real.

Gate 6 (Promise Break) is MANDATORY for every scene.

---

## Story Precedence

The story window remains primary. Counter-narrative scenes don't UNDERMINE the story — they provide the pressure the story exists within. The dissenter's perspective makes the protagonist's choices more meaningful, not less.

## The Counter-Narrative Principle

Counter-narrative scenes are NOT villains. They're valid perspectives. The person who mourns the old version of this world isn't wrong. The person who profits from the new version and knows it's hollow isn't a cartoon. The child who asks the question nobody can answer isn't cute — they're devastating. The friction is BETWEEN legitimate positions, not between right and wrong.

**The best counter-narrative scenes make the audience uncomfortable with their own position.** If the viewer has been rooting for the commons, show them the person the commons displaced. If they've been rooting for the original, show them the 340 people who do genuine good under the new system. The counter-narrative exists to complicate, not resolve.

## Counter-Narrative Lenses

### THE NOSTALGIST

Someone who remembers the world BEFORE the premise and mourns what was lost. Not sentimental — specific. They remember a detail, a smell, a routine, a relationship that the new system erased. Their nostalgia is for something real, not something idealized. The scene is the gap between what they remember and what exists now.

### THE SKEPTIC

Someone who sees through the system — not from a position of power but from a position of clarity. The cab driver who drives between all factions and trusts none of them. The accountant who sees the numbers behind the mythology. The journalist who's filed three stories that were killed. The skeptic's scene is the moment their clarity becomes visible to someone else.

### THE DISPLACED

Someone who was pushed out by the world's premise. The resident priced out by the mythology. The worker replaced by the system. The identity that was absorbed into the commons against their will. The displaced person's scene is always spatial — they are in a place that used to be theirs and isn't anymore, or they are OUTSIDE a place that used to include them.

### THE PROFITEER

Someone who benefits from the system and knows exactly what it costs. Not secretly — OPENLY. The profiteer understands the machinery better than anyone because they built part of it or they feed on it. Their scene is the moment of lucidity: the price stated plainly, the math laid out, the moral compromise named rather than hidden.

### THE INHERITOR

Someone who arrived AFTER the premise was established and has no memory of the before. To them, the world's premise is just... the world. They don't feel the loss because they never had the original. Their innocence is the counter-narrative: the system's success is measured by the generation that doesn't know it was ever different. The scene is the inheritor encountering evidence of the before and not understanding its weight.

### THE APOSTATE

Someone who was inside the system — a true believer — and left. Not fired, not expelled. LEFT. The apostate's scene is the residue: what they still carry from the system they rejected. The badge they kept. The habit they can't break. The language they still use. Apostasy is never clean.

### THE CHILD'S QUESTION

Children ask the questions adults have learned not to ask. The child's counter-narrative is the unfiltered question: "Why?" "Is this real?" "Who decides?" "What happened before?" The child doesn't have a position — they have curiosity, and curiosity is more dangerous than dissent.

### THE NON-HUMAN WITNESS

The building that saw every era. The animal that doesn't understand the system. The weather that treats the managed and unmanaged world identically. The object that outlived its context. Non-human witnesses provide counter-narrative without ideology — their perspective strips the human argument to its material reality.

## World Media Scenes in Counter-Narrative

Counter-narrative generates distinct media artifacts:

- **Dissent media**: the op-ed, the protest sign, the leaked memo, the anonymous post, the oral history
- **Nostalgia media**: the before-and-after photograph, the archived footage, the "I remember when" interview, the vintage advertisement
- **Institutional self-awareness**: the internal audit, the resignation letter, the compliance report that reveals its own absurdity, the training video that accidentally admits the system's flaw
- **Generational contrast**: the textbook that teaches the premise as fact vs. the elder who lived the before, side by side

## Mix Target

~50 scenes. Follow the friction. Some worlds have deep nostalgia veins (worlds built on transformation of a previous order). Some have strong skeptic energy (worlds with visible institutional machinery). Some have devastating displacement stories (worlds where the premise has winners and losers). Let the world's specific tensions determine the mix.

## Anti-Patterns (Invocation 5 Specific)

| Anti-Pattern | Signal |
|---|---|
| **Straw man dissent** | The counter-narrative voice is dumb, cartoonish, or easily dismissed. If the audience doesn't feel the pull of the dissenter's position, the scene fails. |
| **All anger, no grief** | Dissent isn't always loud. The quietest counter-narrative — the person who just left, the person who just stopped caring, the person who remembers — is often the most powerful. |
| **Repeating Invocation 3** | Weird Territory already explored unseen corners. Counter-narrative is about POSITION, not perspective tricks. The lens is ideological, not formal. |
| **Resolving the tension** | Counter-narrative scenes should OPEN questions, not close them. If the scene has an answer, it's an essay. If it has a question, it's a scene. |
| **Forgetting the world's texture** | Counter-narrative scenes still need the world's specific materials, surfaces, and objects. The nostalgia is for a SPECIFIC detail, not a general feeling. The displacement is from a SPECIFIC place. Specificity is everything. |
